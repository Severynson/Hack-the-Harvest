
Crop Production 

ISSN: 1936-3737

Released November 10, 2026, by the National Agricultural Statistics Service 
(NASS), Agricultural Statistics Board, United States Department of 
Agriculture (USDA).

This report was approved on November 10, 2026.

Corn production for grain is forecast at 15.9 billion bushels, down 1 percent from the previous forecast and down 
6 percent from 2025. If realized, this would be the second highest grain production on record for the Nation. The 
average yield is forecast at 179.6 bushels per acre, down 1.6 bushels from the previous forecast and down 6.9 bushels 
from 2025.

Soybean production for beans is forecast at a record high 4.57 billion bushels, up less than 1 percent from the 
previous forecast and up 7 percent from 2025. Yields are expected to average a record high 53.3 bushels per acre, 
up 0.2 bushel from the previous forecast and up 0.3 bushel from 2025.

All cotton production is forecast at 13.2 million 480-pound bales, down 2 percent from the previous forecast and 
down 5 percent from 2025. The average yield is forecast at 775 pounds per harvested acre, down 15 pounds from the 
previous forecast and down 77 pounds from 2025.

The current corn and soybean production forecasts are based on information collected before harvest was complete, 
so the final numbers may change as more information becomes available. Based on 20 years of actual results, there 
is a 90% chance that the final corn production will be between 15.5 billion bushels and 16.3 billion bushels, and 
the final soybean production will fall between 4.43 billion bushels and 4.71 billion bushels.

October Weather Summary

Highlights: Weather impacts related to a strong El Niño further amplified 
during October, as a swath of wetness covered most areas west of a line from 
central Texas to Lake Superior, excluding northern California and the 
Pacific Northwest. Ongoing tropical activity over the eastern Pacific Ocean 
contributed to the enhanced precipitation, as remnant moisture associated 
with former Hurricanes Rachel, Simon, Trudy, and Vance was drawn 
northeastward in advance of various weather systems crossing the western 
United States. In the Four Corners region, two late-month periods of locally 
excessive rainfall, little over a week apart, led to some of the worst 
flooding in decades. Record crests were set along several rivers during both 
events, with high water from late-month downpours persisting into early 
November. Although Western wetness largely suppressed late-season wildfire 
activity, nearly 9.5 million acres had burned during 2026, Nationally, by 
early November, well above the 10-year average of 6.5 million.

In the upper Midwest, harvest activities were slowed by frequent rainfall 
events. Meanwhile, delayed crop maturation in the eastern Corn Belt kept the 
harvest pace behind average and also pushed back the start of winter wheat 
planting. By the first day of November, 26% of the Nation's corn and 12% of 
the soybeans remained in the field. On that date, the corn harvest was more 
than 10 percentage points behind the respective 5-year averages in Iowa 
(66 percent harvested), South Dakota (63 percent), and Michigan (43 percent). 
Meanwhile, more than 15 percent of the soybeans had not yet been harvested by 
November 1 in several Midwestern States, including Missouri (79 percent 
harvested), Michigan (81 percent), Ohio (83 percent), and Nebraska 
(84 percent).

Many areas along and east of a line from the western Gulf Coast region to 
Lake Michigan experienced a mostly dry October, with notable exceptions. 
Ongoing warmth and dryness in the mid-South left topsoil moisture rated 
95 percent very short to short by November 1 in Arkansas, according to 
USDA/NASS, along with 87 percent in Louisiana and 81 percent in Mississippi. 
In fact, values above 50 percent very short to short were observed throughout 
the South, extending as far north as Kentucky and Virginia, except in 
Florida. Agricultural impacts related to Southern dryness included poor 
pasture conditions, reduced surface water supplies, and delayed germination 
of fall-sown crops. On October 25, the final USDA/NASS pasture condition 
report of the season indicated that Arkansas led the Nation with 76 percent 
of its pastures rated very poor to poor. A week later, on November 1, 
Arkansas noted that only 23 percent of its winter wheat had emerged, compared 
to the 5-year average of 36 percent.

According to the U.S. Drought Monitor, drought covered 43.98 percent of the 
Lower 48 States on November 3, down from 59.38 percent on September 29-a 
decrease of 15.40 percentage points in 5 weeks. Despite the improvement, 
November 3 marked the 60th consecutive week with National drought coverage 
topping 40 percent, the longest such streak since there were 126 consecutive 
such weeks from September 2020 - February 2023. As November began, extreme to 
exceptional drought (D3 to D4) covered 82 percent of Arkansas, along with 
53 percent of Louisiana, 38 percent of Mississippi, and 29 percent of 
Tennessee.

Late in the month, persistent onshore flow across Florida led to gusty winds, 
localized downpours, and flash flooding, especially along and near the 
Atlantic Coast. Elsewhere in the East, any rain largely fell during the first 
half of the month and included moisture associated with Isaias, the first 
Atlantic Basin hurricane of 2026. Hurricane Isaias made landfall late 
October 9 near Destin, Florida, with sustained winds near 105 mph. (Isaias 
was also the latest-ever initial Atlantic Basin hurricane of the season 
during the satellite era, supplanting the previous record by 26 days.) 
Significant wind damage related to Isaias was mostly limited to western 
Florida and environs, although heavy showers directly or indirectly 
associated with the former hurricane covered much of the Southeast. Once 
inland, Isaias rapidly weakened before dissipating over the central 
Appalachians on October 11. Elsewhere, generally drier-than-normal October 
weather prevailed in the lower Midwest, Northeast, Pacific Northwest, middle 
Atlantic coastal plain. In those regions, fieldwork advanced with few delays, 
although topsoil moisture in agricultural regions was rated more than 
50 percent very short to short on November 1 in several States, including 
Oregon (73 percent), Washington (57 percent), and Maine (55 percent).

A prominent October feature was overarching warmth, which led to monthly 
temperatures averaging at least 4 to 8°F above normal across large sections 
of the Plains, Midwest, and mid-South. The warmth continued a long stretch of 
warmer-than-normal weather across much of the Nation, as earlier this year 
the country had experienced its warmest March, July, August, and September on 
record. Near- or slightly below-normal October temperatures were confined to 
a few areas, including much of New England and parts of the 
Pacific Northwest. The mid-autumn warmth allowed most later-developing crops 
to reach maturity prior to a season-ending freeze; in fact, areas of the 
Midwest and mid-Atlantic that escaped a mid-October cool spell without a 
freeze experienced an extended growing season.

Crop Comments

Corn: The 2026 area harvested for grain is forecast at 88.5 million acres, 
unchanged from the previous estimate but down 3 percent from last year.

Production for grain is forecast at 15.9 billion bushels, down 1 percent from 
the previous forecast and down 6 percent from last year. If realized, this 
would be the second highest production for grain on record for the 
United States. Based on conditions as of November 1, the yield is forecast at 
179.6 bushels per acre, down 1.6 bushels from the previous forecast and down 
6.9 bushels from last year's record high 186.5 bushels per acre. Record high 
yields are forecast in Arkansas, Iowa, Minnesota, Mississippi, Ohio, 
Tennessee, and Virginia.

Ninety-five percent of the Nation's corn crop was mature by October 18, 
one percentage point behind the 5-year average. On October 25, 
fifty-six percent of the Nation's corn crop was rated in good to excellent 
condition. Seventy-four percent of the 2026 corn acreage had been harvested 
by November 1, three percentage points behind the 5-year average.

Sorghum: Production is forecast at 274 million bushels, down 6 percent from 
the previous forecast and down 37 percent from last year. Based on November 1 
conditions, yield is forecast at 52.4 bushels per acre, 3.3 bushels below the 
previous forecast and 20.2 bushels below the 2025 yield of 72.6 bushels per 
acre. 

By November 1, ninety-six percent of the Nation's sorghum acreage was mature, 
4 percentage points behind the 5-year average. Sixty-four percent of the 
acreage had been harvested at that time, 14 percentage points behind the 5-
year average. 

Rice: All rice production is forecast at 158 million cwt, down slightly from 
the previous forecast and down 24 percent from the previous year. Area for 
harvest is expected to total 2.06 million acres, unchanged from the previous 
estimate and down 25 percent from last year. Based on conditions as of 
November 1, the average United States yield is forecast at 7,664 pounds per 
acre, down 4 pounds per acre from the previous forecast but up 120 pounds per 
acre from 2025.

As of October 18, ninety-seven percent of the Nation's rice acreage had been 
harvested, 4 percentage points ahead of the 5-year average.

Soybeans: Production is forecast at a record 4.57 billion bushels, up less 
than 1 percent from the previous estimate and up 7 percent from last year. 
The forecasted yield, at a record 53.3 bushels per acre, is up 0.3 bushel 
from last year's final estimate. Acreage harvested for beans is forecast at 
85.9 million acres, unchanged from the previous forecast but up 7 percent 
from last year.

The November objective yield data for the 11 major soybean-producing States 
(Arkansas, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, 
North Dakota, Ohio, and South Dakota) indicate a lower pod count compared 
with the previous year. Compared with final counts for 2025, pod counts are 
down in 6 of the 11 published States. Kansas showed the greatest decrease, 
down 358 pods per 18 square feet from the previous year.

As of November 1, eighty-eight percent of the United States soybean acreage 
was harvested, 4 percentage points ahead of the 5-year average. At that time, 
harvest progress was at or ahead of the respective State 5-year average pace 
in 15 of the 18 States estimated in the Crop Progress report.

If realized, the forecasted yield will be a record high in Arkansas, 
Delaware, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Mississippi, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and 
Tennessee.

Peanuts: Production is forecast at 4.93 billion pounds in 2026, down 
4 percent from the previous forecast and down 31 percent from last year. Area 
harvested is expected to total 1.36 million acres, unchanged from the 
previous forecast but down 28 percent from 2025. Based on conditions as of 
November 1, the average yield for the United States is forecast at 
3,627 pounds per acre, down 139 pounds per acre from the previous forecast 
and down 119 pounds per acre from 2025.

Cotton: All cotton production is forecast at 13.2 million 480-pound bales, 
down 2 percent from the previous forecast and down 5 percent from 2025. Based 
on conditions as of November 1, yields are expected to average 775 pounds per 
harvested acre, down 15 pounds from the previous forecast and down 77 pounds 
from 2025. Upland cotton production is forecast at 12.8 million 480-pound 
bales, down 2 percent from the previous forecast and down 5 percent from 
2025. Pima cotton production is forecast at 381,000 bales, down 1 percent 
from the previous month and down 6 percent from 2025. 

If realized, the forecasted yield will be a record high in Arkansas

On November 1, ninety-two percent of the Nation's cotton acreage had bolls 
opening, three percentage points behind the 5-year average. 
Sixty-three percent of the acreage had been harvested, 9 percentage points 
ahead of the 5-year average. 

Ginnings totaled 3,701,850 running bales prior to November 1.

Sugarbeets: Production of sugarbeets for the 2026 crop year is forecast at 
30.1 million tons, down 3 percent from last month and down 14 percent from 
last year. Producers expect to harvest 1.01 million acres, unchanged from the 
previous estimate but down 5 percent from last year. Yield is forecast at 
29.8 tons per acre, down 1.0 ton from the previous estimate and down 3.4 tons 
from last year.

Sugarcane: Production for sugar and seed is estimated at 33.2 million tons, 
down 1 percent from last month and down 4 percent from 2025. Producers intend 
to harvest 944,000 acres for sugar and seed during the 2026 crop year, 
unchanged from the previous estimate but down slightly from last season. 
Yields for sugar and seed are estimated to average 35.2 tons per acre, down 
0.1 ton from last month and down 1.2 tons from 2025. Record high production 
for sugarcane for sugar and seed is estimated in Louisiana.

Potatoes: Production of potatoes for the 2026 crop year is forecast at 
388 million cwt, down 7 percent from last year. Planted area, at 
886,000 acres, is up 1 percent from the June estimate but down 2 percent from 
last season. Area harvested, at 880,900 acres, is up 2 percent from the 
previous forecast but down 2 percent from the previous year. Area planted and 
area harvested both represent the lowest acreage on record for the 
United States. Based on conditions as of November 1, the yield is forecast at 
440 cwt per acre, down 22 cwt from last year's record high yield.

Record high yields are forecast in Colorado.

Statistical Methodology

Field crop survey procedures: Objective yield and farm operator surveys were 
conducted between October 24 and November 4 to gather information on expected 
yield as of November 1. The objective yield surveys for corn, cotton, and 
soybeans were conducted in the major producing States that usually account 
for about 75 percent of the United States production. Randomly selected plots 
were revisited to make current counts. The counts made within each sample 
plot depend on the crop and the maturity of that crop. In all cases, plant 
counts are recorded along with other measurements that provide information to 
forecast the number of ears, bolls, or pods and their weight. The counts are 
used with similar data from previous years to develop a projected biological 
yield. The average harvesting loss is subtracted to obtain a net yield. The 
plots are visited starting in September and are revisited each month until 
crop maturity when the fruit is harvested and weighed. After the farm 
operator has harvested the sample field, another plot is sampled to obtain 
current year harvesting loss.

The farm operator survey was conducted primarily by telephone with some use 
of mail, internet, and personal interviewers. Approximately 6,300 producers 
were interviewed during the survey period and asked questions about probable 
yield.

Field crop estimating procedures: The Agricultural Statistics Board (ASB) 
reviews multiple data sources at the National and State level, including USDA 
administrative acreage data, satellite imagery, and objective yield and 
grower reported data. All data were reviewed for reasonableness and 
consistency with historical estimates. The data were also reviewed 
considering weather patterns and crop progress compared with previous months 
and previous years.

Revision policy: The November 1 production forecast will not be revised; 
instead, a new forecast will be made each month throughout the growing 
season. End-of-season estimates are made after harvest. At the end of the 
marketing season, a balance sheet is calculated using carryover stocks, 
production, exports, millings, feeding, and ending stocks. Revisions are then 
made if the balance sheet relationships or other administrative data warrant 
changes. Estimates of acres for barley, corn, cotton, dry edible beans, oats, 
peanuts, rice, sorghum, soybeans, sugarbeets, and wheat are subject to 
revision in the August Crop Production report. Acres for chickpeas, corn, 
cotton, dry edible peas, lentils, peanuts, rice, sorghum, soybeans, and 
sugarbeets are subject to revision in the September Crop Production report 
each year. Barley, oats, rye, and wheat end-of-season estimates are published 
in the Small Grains Annual Summary report at the end of September. Canola, 
dry edible beans, and sunflower acres are subject to revision in the October 
Crop Production report. Potato acres are subject to revision in the November 
Crop Production report. End-of-season estimates for all other row crops are 
published in the Annual Crop Production Summary in January.  Revisions to 
planted acres will only be made when either special survey data, 
administrative data, such as Farm Service Agency certified acreage data, or 
remote sensing data are available. Harvested acres may be revised any time a 
production forecast is made if there is sufficient data indicating that the 
intended harvested area has changed since the last forecast.

Reliability: To assist users in evaluating the reliability of the November 1 
production forecast, the "Root Mean Square Error," a statistical measure 
based on past performance, is computed. The deviation between the November 1 
production forecast and the final estimate is expressed as a percentage of 
the final estimate. The average of the squared percentage deviations for the 
latest 20-year period is computed. The square root of the average becomes 
statistically the "Root Mean Square Error." Probability statements can be 
made concerning expected differences in the current forecast relative to the 
final end-of-season estimate, assuming that factors affecting this year's 
forecast are not different from those influencing recent years. For example, 
the "Root Mean Square Error" for the November 1 corn for grain production 
forecast is 1.3 percent. This means that chances are 2 out of 3 that the 
current production forecast will not be above or below the final estimate by 
more than 1.3 percent. Chances are 9 out of 10 (90 percent confidence level) 
that the difference will not exceed 2.3 percent.
